The ground we stand on

These are the commitments that shape our commentary and our work with churches and communities. They are held as Christian convictions, offered as a positive vision for Australia, and open to serious conversation.

Faith and freedom of religion

Australians should be free to believe, to worship, and to speak about faith in public as well as in private. That freedom belongs to Christians and equally to people of other faiths and of none. A confident country does not ask people of faith to leave their convictions at the door of the school, the workplace or the public square.

We will speak for the freedom to live as a Christian in ordinary Australian life: to raise children in the faith, to gather as churches, and to take part in public conversation without being pushed to the edge of it. We ask of others the courtesy we hope to receive.

Freedom of religion is not a demand for special treatment. It is part of a free society, and section 116 of the Constitution already recognises that the Commonwealth must not prohibit the free exercise of religion.

Family

Marriage, parenthood and stable households are a gift to children and a foundation for the wider community. We hold that a lifelong marriage between a man and a woman is the setting Scripture and long Christian teaching give for family life, and that children flourish when they are known, protected and loved.

Families come under strain, and many homes are carrying real pain. We have no interest in shaming people who are hurting. We do want public life to honour parents, to make room for raising children, and to treat the family as something worth protecting.

Strong households are not a private hobby. They are one of the ways a society cares for the next generation.

The dignity of human life

Every person is made in the image of God. That dignity does not depend on age, ability, usefulness or independence. It covers the child in the womb, the person with disability, the elderly, and the neighbour who is easy to overlook.

Because life is given, we believe it should be protected at every stage. We want Australia to be a country where vulnerable people are cared for. That conviction shapes how we speak about abortion, euthanasia and disability: with clarity, and with compassion for people who are suffering or afraid.

We will not talk about these questions as if they were only abstract. They are about particular people, and those people are owed both truth and kindness.

Truth and moral courage

Public life needs people who will tell the truth when it is unpopular, and who will do it without cruelty. Christian conviction is not a licence to sneer. It is a call to integrity: say what you mean, keep your word, and refuse to twist the truth to win an argument.

Young Australians are growing up in a noisy culture. We want to encourage them to think clearly, to listen properly, and to speak with grace when something matters. Courage and kindness belong together.

We would rather be plain and answerable than clever and slippery. If we get something wrong, we should say so.

Community and service

Christian faith is not only private. It shows up in how we treat the person next to us: in practical help, in the life of a local church, and in ordinary civic responsibility. Loving your neighbour is time, money, patience and attention given to real people in a real place.

We want churches, ministries and households to be known for service, both to their own people and to the wider community. Australia is better when people take responsibility for one another.

If you can host, volunteer, offer a skill, or simply pray, that is part of this work. Tell us how you can help.

Responsible citizenship and a fair go

The Australian idea of a fair go belongs with a Christian view of the person: each one has dignity, and each one is called to responsibility. Work, honesty and keeping your commitments are good things. So is a public culture that does not crush initiative, and that does not walk past people who need a hand.

We encourage the next generation to take part: to vote, to speak, to serve locally, and to stay engaged when public debate is frustrating. Citizenship is a duty as well as a right.

We hold that duty with humility about our own limits. We are advocates and neighbours, not rulers of anyone else’s conscience.
